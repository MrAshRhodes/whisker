Your Whisker guide.

Whisker is a menu bar app that changes what your mouse's buttons, wheel and pointer do, with profiles for each app and each mouse. This guide covers every setting.

Getting started

Whisker needs macOS 14 Sonoma or later. Releases are signed with an Apple Developer ID and notarised by Apple, and Gatekeeper stays on.

  1. Install Whisker with Homebrew: 
    brew install --cask mrashrhodes/tap/whisker
    Or download the DMG or ZIP from the latest release and check it against its .sha256 file; the release notes show how. Open the DMG or unzip the ZIP, then move Whisker.app to your Applications folder. If macOS refuses to open it, stop and report it rather than bypassing the warning.
  2. Open Whisker from your Applications folder or Spotlight.

The first time Whisker opens, the Welcome to Whisker window explains the one permission it needs. Choose Open Accessibility Settings, turn Whisker on in the list, and you're done. You can also tick Open Whisker at login there. Continue Without Access lets you look around first.

Prefer to build it yourself? You need Xcode, or just the Command Line Tools with xcode-select --install, and Swift 5.9 or later. Run these commands, then move dist/Whisker.app to your Applications folder and open it. (make run builds and opens it in one step.)

git clone https://github.com/MrAshRhodes/whisker.git
cd whisker
make app

Permissions

Accessibility (required)

Accessibility access lets Whisker see mouse buttons and wheel turns before apps do, and post the clicks, keys and scrolls you've mapped them to. It doesn't read what you type, and nothing leaves your Mac.

To grant it, open System Settings › Privacy & Security › Accessibility and turn on Whisker, or choose Grant Accessibility Access… from Whisker's menu. Whisker checks every second while it's running, so it starts working as soon as access is on, even if you closed the welcome window.

To take access away, turn Whisker off in the same list. Whisker stops changing your mouse straight away and lets go of any button or key it was holding. Whether that includes a held Click Lock has not been verified.

Input Monitoring (optional)

Needed if you use two or more mice and want settings for each one, or want to run an Input timing sample or Extra button discovery. These opt-in diagnostics need it even with one mouse; ordinary single-mouse mappings do not. Turn it on from Settings › Devices › Telling Mice Apart › Allow Input Monitoring…. Opening a mouse's details never asks for it, and the diagnostic windows ask only when you choose Allow Input Monitoring….

A copy you built yourself is signed only for your Mac, so each rebuild looks like a new app to macOS. If Whisker stops responding after a rebuild, remove it from the Accessibility list with the − button and add it again.

Devices

Settings › Devices lists the mice and trackballs Whisker has seen, under Connected and Not Connected, with each one's name and vendor and product numbers.

  • Make Profile creates a profile for that mouse. Edit Buttons opens its mappings.
  • Settings for a single mouse, such as pointer speed and scroll direction, follow that mouse wherever you plug it in.
  • Trackpads and touch mice are listed too, as Trackpad — left alone and Touch mouse — left alone (Magic Mouse is one). Whisker doesn't change their buttons, scrolling or pointer.
  • Choose Details under any device to see what Whisker knows about it.
  • Choose Input Timing… beside a connected mouse to run an opt-in timing sample.

Details

Choose Details under a device to open a read-only summary. Opening it changes no setting, doesn't ask for Input Monitoring and never writes to the device. For a mouse that isn't connected it shows only what its saved settings record.

  • Who it is: Product name, Connection (such as USB or Bluetooth), Vendor ID and Product ID. Anything a device doesn't report says Not reported, and a device without a name is listed by its ids.
  • Serial number: whether the device reports one. The number stays hidden until you choose Show, and it's hidden again when you close the details. Whisker already uses it as part of the mouse's identity in your settings, and saves nothing more.
  • Input Monitoring and Attribution are separate. Input Monitoring says whether Whisker can listen for button and wheel reports: Allowed, Not allowed or Allowed, but Whisker couldn't start listening. Attribution says how sure Whisker is which mouse a click came from: Recent matching input (that mouse sent a button press or wheel turn in the last minute), Inferred from one eligible mouse (it's the only mouse Whisker handles, so clicks are assumed to be its), Ambiguous identity (several connected devices look the same) or Unknown. It's evidence, not a guarantee, and a mouse can be allowed yet Unknown until you use it.
  • Reported buttons and Reported wheels: what the device tells macOS it can send, each listed once. This isn't a count of physical buttons, and None reported or Unavailable never means there are none. Whisker reads them once Input Monitoring is allowed and doesn't ask for it just to show them.
  • Shared settings: mice with the same vendor and product ids and no serial number have the same identity, so they share one set of settings, and the details say how many connected devices share it. Whisker can only notice this when each device reports where it's connected: two such mice that don't are listed as one. A mouse that reports a serial number is kept apart from the others.
  • Left alone: a trackpad or touch mouse's details say why Whisker leaves it alone, and it isn't counted as a second mouse.
  • Privacy: Whisker keeps only when each mouse last sent a button press or wheel turn, in memory, and treats it as recent for 60 seconds. It doesn't keep what was sent or a history, never saves, logs or sends it anywhere, and forgets it when the mouse is unplugged or Whisker quits. Details add nothing to the settings file.
  • Battery (best effort): shown here, and beside the mouse's name when there's a percentage. Whisker only reads what the mouse itself declares through macOS's public HID interface, once Input Monitoring is allowed. It tries two sources, the standard Generic Device Controls Battery Strength element and the Battery System Absolute State of Charge element, and shows a percentage only when the element's range is exactly 0 to 100. Charging appears only when the mouse declares an explicit charging flag, never guessed from USB or a rising level. Whisker never uses the Mac's own battery or a UPS, never writes to the mouse and never asks for a permission for this. The row can say:
    • Not supported: the mouse declares no battery element.
    • Waiting for a reading: the mouse has one and Whisker hasn't received a value yet.
    • Unavailable — …: Input Monitoring is off, macOS didn't describe the device, a read failed, the value was out of range, the battery is in a format Whisker doesn't recognise, or Whisker can't tell which device the battery belongs to (identical mice without serial numbers, a wireless receiver or several battery elements). It never shows another mouse's reading.
    • Stale — 80%, last read 10:42: the last good reading is older than 15 minutes, or a later read failed.
    • Disconnected: the mouse isn't connected.
    • A percentage, optionally with charging or not charging.
    Readings refresh when the mouse reports a new value, when you open Details and at most every ten minutes for a couple of hours after it connects. They stay in memory and are never saved. If the row says anything but a percentage, check the battery in the Bluetooth menu or Control Centre instead.

Not yet verified on hardware: reported buttons and wheels. In one test on macOS 26.6 with Input Monitoring off, macOS gave Whisker no controls for any of twelve built-in, USB and Bluetooth interfaces, so every mouse showed Unavailable. Other versions of macOS, other connections and what you see once Input Monitoring is allowed haven't been tried, and neither has how closely Recent matching input follows real use.

Not yet verified on hardware: battery status. No mouse or receiver model has been verified to supply a reading, including Magic Mouse (which Whisker leaves alone, and whose battery macOS shows in its own menus), so don't expect a reading from any particular model.

Duplicate clicks

An advanced filter, off by default, for a mouse that sometimes sends a second click straight after an intended one. Open Duplicate clicks (advanced) under a mouse, tick the buttons to filter and choose how short a repeat counts, from 20 to 100 ms (50 ms to start).

  • A press that comes within that time of the previous click's release, and is let go within that time itself, is dropped together with its release. Nothing is ever dropped without its pair, and a press that has gone through always gets its release.
  • The first click is never delayed. A press that might be a repeat is held for at most the interval, and a drag, a longer hold or any other input lets it through first, in order. Clicks that go through after a dropped repeat carry the count the app would have seen without it.
  • It can ignore clicks you meant. Timing alone can't tell chatter from a deliberate fast double-click, so one quicker than your interval is ignored too. This is not the double-click speed in System Settings, which only changes how close together two clicks must be to count as a double-click.
  • It only works for a mouse Whisker can identify exactly (see Input Monitoring with several mice), only for the buttons you tick, and never for the wheel or other mice. A left click with no modifier key is only filtered while Allow remapping the primary button is on in General.
  • Turning it on starts a 60-second trial: it switches itself off unless you press Keep. Turn Off Filter is an ordinary button, reachable with the keyboard and VoiceOver. Turning Whisker off, an app on the bypass list coming forward, changing settings, unplugging the mouse or macOS pausing Whisker clears anything being held.

This has not yet been verified on real faulty hardware, nor for Finder double-clicks, text selection or other apps. Whisker does not diagnose or repair a mouse.

Extra button discovery

Settings › Devices › Extra Button Discovery… is stage 1: a read-only diagnostic, not extra-button remapping. It is off by default. Enable discovery for the mouse, then choose Inspect each time. The saved preference never starts listening automatically. Input Monitoring is required even with one mouse; denial or revocation stops discovery with a reason and leaves ordinary mappings unchanged.

  • Labels, not compatibility claims. Collections and Button (0x09)/Consumer (0x0C) elements appear with a hid.generic report/page/usage identity, separate from ordinary CGEvent buttons 0–31. Observed means a value arrived, including zero/releases. Not yet observed does not prove a control works. Unsupported and Ambiguous mean metadata only. Descriptor discovery is not vendor-report decoding.
  • Strict attribution. Only one direct Bluetooth pointing interface with a unique serial-backed identity and safe binary elements can supply values. USB devices (including wired mice), shared receivers, identical or missing identities, duplicate descriptor identities and uncertain combinations remain metadata-only. The sole listed mouse or a receiver identity does not prove which paired mouse sent a control.
  • Keyboard privacy. Related keyboard interfaces are counted from metadata, never opened by discovery. Keyboard/Keypad (0x07), nested keyboard collections, primary-button values, arrays and non-binary controls are never subscribed to. Trackpads and touch mice are untouched.
  • Read-only. A private, non-exclusive, filtered HID value queue listens only to eligible mouse elements. Discovery sends no reports, installs no report callback, diverts no control and changes no device setting or ordinary mapping. Native effects still happen: these controls stay diagnostic-only.
  • Cleared, not resumed. Stop, closing the window, disabling discovery or Whisker, settings or active app/profile changes, sleep, user switching, lost permission and disconnect/reconnect clear the observations. Nothing resumes without Inspect. Observations stay in memory, never saved or logged, with a 64-value queue and 128 recent transitions per interface, at most 512 descriptor elements and eight interfaces. This is best-effort evidence, not a lossless recorder or a guarantee of current button state. There are no held actions or changed device settings to restore.

Stage 2 is unavailable. No vendor adapter is approved, and no model, firmware or transport combination has been verified on hardware. Vendor remapping needs documented provenance/version, verified hardware, suppression of native effects and tested rollback/crash/reconnect recovery. None is claimed here.

The per-device extraButtons.discoveryEnabled preference defaults to false. The separate extraButtonMappings object is opaque, reserved and never executed. Existing mappings and unknown top-level settings keys are preserved, but unknown keys inside typed nested settings are still discarded. The wider unknown-key criterion remains unmet; the schema stays at version 2.

Input timing

A short, opt-in diagnostic for one mouse at a time. It counts when that mouse's reports reach Whisker, so you can see whether they arrive steadily or in bursts. It does nothing until you press Start, changes no setting and never writes to the mouse.

  1. Choose Input Timing… beside a connected mouse in Settings › Devices. If Input Monitoring is off, the window says so and offers Allow Input Monitoring…; after allowing it in System Settings, come back to the window.
  2. Press Start, then move the mouse in steady circles for about ten seconds. Don't click or scroll, so that the sample is only movement. A bar shows the seconds and the reports so far, and Cancel stops it.
  3. Read the result, or press Run Again. Closing the window forgets it.
  • What you see. For each stream of reports the mouse sends: how many arrived, about how many a second, and the gaps between them while the mouse was moving (shortest, median, 95th percentile and longest, with the 99th percentile only when there are enough gaps for it to mean anything). A pause of more than 100 ms is counted and kept out of the while the mouse was moving figures. Anything odd is listed in plain words, such as reports that repeated an earlier timestamp, arrived out of order, had no timestamp or fell outside the sample, and none of those is counted.
  • Streams stay apart. A mouse, or a wireless receiver, can send reports on more than one interface or report ID. Each stream has its own box, labelled Interface 0, or Interface 0 · report ID 2 when it has a report ID, and the figures are never added together. Two mice are never mixed: it listens to the mouse you chose.
  • When there is nothing to show. A mouse that was hardly moved gets Not enough movement to measure, one that sent nothing gets No reports arrived on this stream during the sample, and a stream whose timestamps don't line up with the sample shows no figures at all. Whisker never shows a rate of zero, or one it had to guess.
  • Privacy. It records when each report arrived and nothing else: not what a report contained, where the pointer went or anything you typed. The times stay in memory, and are never saved, logged or sent anywhere. Input Timing listens only while a sample runs. Cancel, closing the window, the Mac going to sleep or switching user, Input Monitoring being turned off, or the mouse being unplugged or changing all stop the listening and throw the sample away; the window says so instead of showing a result.
  • Only listening. Whisker listens to the mouse's own mouse or pointer interfaces through a connection of its own, opened without taking the device over, so macOS and other apps keep receiving the mouse as before. It never reads what a report contains, writes to the device or changes a property of it, and a sample leaves Whisker's button remapping, scrolling and mouse identification as they were. A keyboard interface on the same device is never opened, and an interface whose reports mix movement with other input is skipped rather than timed as if it were movement alone.
  • When it won't start. Trackpads and touch mice are left alone, so there is no Input Timing… for them. For a mouse, the window says why when it can't time it: it isn't connected, Input Monitoring is off, it is one of several identical mice with no serial number (so Whisker can't tell whose reports are whose), macOS hasn't described it, it reports nothing from a mouse or pointer, its reports mix movement with other input, or macOS wouldn't let Whisker open it.

It isn't a polling rate. Input timing counts only the reports macOS delivered to Whisker for that mouse, and the time macOS stamped on each. It is not the mouse's polling rate, not latency from the mouse to the screen, not packet loss and not the rate at which a game or another app reads the mouse. Whisker can't see how often the mouse could send a report, which ones never arrived, how long they took or what another app does with them. Use the figures to compare one mouse, connection or receiver with another, not as a specification.

Not yet verified on hardware: Input timing hasn't been tried on a real mouse, wired, through a wireless receiver or over Bluetooth. What the timestamps measure on each, their units and how a receiver delivers its reports are unconfirmed, and no mouse or receiver model is known to work. The sample length, the 20 short gaps it needs and the 100 ms pause limit are provisional.

Buttons

Settings › Buttons shows a profile's own mappings under Mappings, and the ones it takes from Default under Inherited.

A mapping is a button (or a wheel direction), an exact set of modifier keys, and an action. Button 4 on its own and ⌥ + Button 4 are two separate mappings. The modifier keys are ⌘ ⌥ ⌃ and ⇧.

  1. Choose Record Button…, then press the mouse button or turn the wheel. Esc cancels.
  2. Pick the modifier keys to hold, if any, and an action.
  3. Choose Add.

Buttons are numbered the way macOS reports them: primary, secondary, middle, then Button 4, Button 5 and so on, up to 32. Out of the box, Button 4 goes Back and Button 5 goes Forward.

So that you can always click, the primary button can only be remapped with a modifier key held. Settings › General › Safety › Allow remapping the primary button lifts that limit; take care with it.

If an action relies on a macOS keyboard shortcut that's switched off, Whisker warns you and offers Open Keyboard Shortcuts.

Tilt-wheel controls

A wheel that tilts sideways may reach macOS as a numbered button or as horizontal scrolling, and which one depends on the mouse and its driver. Whisker doesn't assume either. Identify tilt-wheel controls… in Settings › Buttons asks you to tilt left, tilt right and press the wheel, shows what macOS actually reported for each, and saves only what you confirm.

  • A numbered button stays an ordinary button mapping, with a normal press and release.
  • Horizontal scrolling stays Scroll Left or Scroll Right. That mapping also matches any other horizontal scrolling from the same mouse, so it can't be limited to the tilt, and a tilt sends no release, so it runs once per tilt and is never held down. Existing once-per-burst and per-notch behaviour is unchanged.
  • Nothing, smooth or coasting scrolling, a fraction of a notch, or sideways and vertical movement together only gets an explanation. Whisker never guesses a button number from them.

The flow names the mouse it believes you used and how sure it is. When exactly one mouse sent input just now it can save a mouse-specific profile and optional labels such as “Wheel tilt left”. When it is only a guess, or several mice are active, you can still save mappings into the selected profile but no hardware label is attached. Labels are notes shown with the mouse: they don't change what a trigger matches or the primary-button safeguard below. A confirmed mapping starts as Pass through, so nothing changes until you pick an action.

Not yet verified on hardware: no tilt-wheel mouse has been tested. Checklist for a mouse you have:

  1. Tilt left and right. Note whether the preview says button or horizontal scrolling, and which direction each tilt gives.
  2. Press the wheel without tilting; it should not be reported as a tilt.
  3. Hold a tilt and see whether it repeats, and whether other horizontal scrolling from the same mouse (a thumb wheel, say) shows as the same trigger.
  4. With a mapping saved, check that plain vertical scrolling and ordinary buttons still work, and that a tilt runs its action once.
  5. Connect a second mouse and check the flow does not claim the wrong one.

Triple Click

Triple Click sends three left clicks in a row at the pointer each time you press the button, with the same optional modifier keys as Double Click. Letting go of the button sends nothing more, so unlike Click it can't be used to drag. On a wheel direction it runs once for each burst of turning, or once for every notch with Repeat for every notch.

  • It's a mouse gesture, not a "select paragraph" command. What three clicks do is up to the app: text fields often use them to select a line or paragraph, while other apps ignore them or do something else. Whisker doesn't promise any particular result.
  • Whisker sends the clicks back to back, as Double Click does. Whether any app needs them spaced out is not yet known, so there is no setting for it.
  • If something cuts a triple click short, such as a click, scroll or button press of your own, a change of app or profile, Whisker being switched off or quit, Whisker losing Accessibility access, the mouse you used being unplugged, your Mac going to sleep or macOS pausing Whisker, Whisker sends no more clicks and doesn't try again. Clicks already sent can't be taken back, so an app may see fewer than three.

This has not yet been verified in TextEdit, Safari or any other app, so which apps respond to it as a triple click is not yet known.

Shortcut Sequences

A Keyboard Shortcut Sequence runs two to sixteen key combinations in order each time you press the button, such as ⌘A then ⌘C. Each step has a delay after it of 0 to 2,000 ms, and the whole sequence can take at most 10 seconds. The delay after the last step is ignored.

  • It runs once per press and never overlaps. Pressing the same mapping again cancels it; pressing a different mapping is ignored until it finishes. Choose Cancel Sequence in the menu to stop it.
  • It is also cancelled by a change of frontmost app (including leaving and coming back), changed settings, Whisker being switched off, bypassed, asleep or quit, Whisker losing Accessibility access, or a reset of the event tap. Keys are never left held down.
  • If you are holding modifier keys that a step doesn't include, or the step's own key, Whisker stops the sequence with a beep rather than send a different shortcut.
  • Delivery is best-effort. Whisker checks the frontmost app before each step, but the app can change between the check and the keystroke, and a change of window within the same app isn't detected.
  • Settings are now saved with schema 2, so Whisker 0.1.0 refuses a file saved by this version.

This has not yet been verified in any real app.

Two-button chords — foundation only

Two-button chords are not active in this build. Buttons shows disabled Record two-button chord… and Primary + secondary → middle click controls with the reason. Nothing is buffered or delayed, and ordinary mappings, primary clicks and Click Lock's primary-button escape are unchanged. Granting Input Monitoring does not enable chords.

Settings can retain a per-profile chords list for future use. Each definition has distinct first and second button numbers, modifiers, an existing action, and a pressWindow in milliseconds. An omitted field inherits the parent's list, or means no chords without a parent; [] disables inherited chords. A list replaces its parent's list without changing ordinary mappings. Pairs cannot share a button, even with different modifiers. Primary-button pairs require keyboard modifiers or Allow remapping the primary button. The middle-click preset uses the existing Click action. Unknown top-level keys remain preserved; unknown nested keys are still not preserved.

The pure model recognises either press order, with a provisional window of 75 ms, bounded to 20–200 ms. Future live recognition would delay eligible single clicks by up to that window. Timeout, early release or drag would resolve the original events through ordinary mappings in order; a recognised middle click would start when both buttons are held, drag, end on the first release and consume the remaining release. These are deterministic model tests, not verified live behaviour or usability recommendations.

Activation remains blocked on reliable physical-connection attribution for both presses and releases (including identical mice and shared receivers), retained Quartz-event replay, timer expiry and shared held-output ownership. Model-level device identity is not sufficient. Input Monitoring may become a prerequisite, but no additional permission is needed for these dormant settings.

Before activation, app/profile/bypass changes, recording, disconnect, sleep, disable, quit and tap interruption must discard pending clicks without replaying into another app and release owned outputs exactly once. Those adapter/recovery paths, recording, and hardware or real-app trials are not implemented or verified by the pure tests. There is no live chord state to recover today; issue #24 remains partial.

Button gestures

A button can run a different action for each way you move the mouse while you hold it: up, down, left or right, plus an optional action for letting go without having moved. It is off for every button until you turn it on, and it is experimental: it has not yet been verified on hardware.

  1. In Settings › Buttons, choose Record Button…, press the button and choose Add, or open the mapping you already have for it.
  2. Turn on Gestures for that mapping. The gesture actions take the place of its own action, which is saved as Default so that a version of Whisker without gestures leaves the button alone.
  3. For Up, Down, Left, Right and Tap (let go without moving), choose Nothing or an action. Until at least one has an action, the button keeps its normal behaviour.
  4. Change the distance if you like. Picks a direction after … units of movement goes from 20 to 400 in steps of 10 and starts at 60.

Then hold the button, move the mouse and let go. A gesture belongs to a mapping, so the modifier keys held when you press the button choose the mapping, and a profile that doesn't set one takes it from the profile it inherits from, like any other mapping.

  • Picking a direction. Whisker adds up how far the mouse you pressed with has moved since you pressed the button, in the movement units macOS reports for it (roughly screen points, so the distance depends on the pointer speed). The first time the total reaches your distance, it picks a direction only if the movement runs mostly one way: at least twice as far along one axis as across it. A diagonal picks nothing, and so do jitter and wiggling back and forth that add up to little. Once a direction is picked, or given up on, moving further doesn't change it.
  • What runs. Letting go runs the action for the direction it picked, once. Letting go with the movement still short of your distance runs Tap, if you set one. A diagonal that reached your distance runs neither, and a direction or a tap left on Nothing does nothing.
  • What apps see. The pointer keeps moving as usual, but the app under it gets no press, release or drag from that button, however the gesture ends. Other buttons, other mappings, the wheel and other mice carry on as normal. A gesture on the primary button follows the same safeguard as any other mapping of it: without a modifier key it needs Allow remapping the primary button, and until that is on a plain primary click is left alone.
  • Which actions. Only actions that are finished once they have run: Double Click, Triple Click, Keyboard Shortcut (without Repeat while held), Back or Forward, Media Key, Mission Control, Switch Space, Switch App, Scroll, and Open App, File or Website. Volume and brightness keys press once rather than repeating. Default, Do Nothing, Click, Hold to Scroll, Auto-scroll, Click Lock, Slow pointer while held, Keyboard Shortcut Sequence, a repeating Keyboard Shortcut and Pointer Speed Preset aren't offered, and Whisker removes one from a settings file that names it. Wheel directions can't have gestures.
  • One at a time. Only one gesture, Hold to Scroll or Auto-scroll can be under way. A second gesture button, a Hold to Scroll button or an Auto-scroll button pressed during a gesture is swallowed and does nothing. Pressing a gesture button while an auto-scroll runs stops the auto-scroll first, as any button press does, and then starts the gesture. A Click Lock owns the pointer's movement too: a gesture button pressed while a lock is held is swallowed and does nothing, and so is a Click Lock button pressed during a gesture.
  • Called off. Whisker runs nothing, and keeps swallowing the button until you let go, if the app in front changes (even if you come straight back), the mouse you pressed with is disconnected, your settings change, Whisker is switched off, stopped or bypassed for the app in front, the Mac sleeps or Whisker quits, you start Record Button…, or Whisker can't tell which mouse the movement came from for three movements in a row.
  • Which mouse. It needs Input Monitoring, so Whisker knows which mouse you pressed and which one is moving. Only the mouse you pressed counts: another mouse's movement never picks a direction, and a release from another mouse never runs the action. When Whisker can't tell which mouse pressed the button, or Input Monitoring is off, the button keeps doing what it normally does.
  • Which buttons. A gesture button has to reach macOS as an ordinary button press and release. Whether a particular one does, such as the gesture button on the Logitech MX Master 3S, is not yet verified, and no mouse is listed as supported. A button macOS doesn't report at all can't be used, and Whisker doesn't talk to a mouse's own software or divert its buttons; support for such buttons is issue #25.
  • Not included. Gestures of any other shape (circles, letters, a path), touch gestures on a trackpad or touch mouse, and changes to the pointer speed. Using movement to scroll is what Hold to Scroll is for.

Not yet verified on hardware: that any real mouse sends a gesture button as an ordinary press and release; how the default distance of 60 and the two-to-one rule feel, and how the distance behaves at different pointer speeds; that an app never sees a stray click or drag from a gesture button; and how the editor works with VoiceOver and the keyboard. The distance and the rules are provisional.

Auto-scroll (Experimental)

Give a button the Auto-scroll (Experimental) action in Settings › Buttons. Press it once with the pointer where you want to start, and let go: that spot is the anchor, and the window under it keeps scrolling by itself, faster the further you move the pointer from the anchor. Below the anchor it scrolls down and above it scrolls up. Both Directions adds left and right, each on its own, while Vertical Only ignores sideways movement. The trigger has to be a mouse button, not a wheel direction, and it can include modifier keys.

  • Dead zone is how far the pointer can stray from the anchor before anything scrolls, from 4 to 120 pt (12 to start). Top speed is from 100 to 6,000 px/s (1,200 to start). The speed rises evenly from nothing at the edge of the dead zone to the top speed 240 pt further out. It follows the clock, not the number of events, so it doesn't depend on how fast the pointer reports or how busy your Mac is. These numbers are provisional.
  • It never starts again by itself. Pressing the same button again, with the same modifier keys, stops it and does nothing else. Pressing any other mouse button stops it too, and that press then does what it normally would, so a click still reaches whatever is under the pointer. That includes swapped buttons, a button that slows the pointer, a button that starts a gesture or a Click Lock, and buttons mapped to other actions. A real turn of the wheel or swipe on a trackpad stops it as well, but the momentum that follows a swipe doesn't.
  • It is also stopped by a change of app, bypass, profile or settings, by Whisker being switched off, by any mouse being connected or disconnected, by a change of Space or display, by your Mac going to sleep or switching user, by Whisker quitting or losing Accessibility access, by macOS pausing Whisker, and by recording a button in Settings. Pressing a different button that is also set to Auto-scroll replaces it with a new anchor, and it can't start while Hold to Scroll is running, a button gesture is under way or a Click Lock is held.
  • There is no marker at the pointer. While it runs, the menu bar icon changes to a circle with an up and down arrow, even if you have hidden the icon, and its tooltip says "Whisker: auto-scroll is on. Click or scroll to stop it."
  • Whisker never moves the pointer. It sends the scrolling to the anchor, and marks it so that Whisker neither remaps it nor counts it as your own scrolling, which would stop it.
  • It needs Accessibility but no Input Monitoring of its own, and follows the pointer whichever mouse moves it. A profile for one mouse still needs Input Monitoring to tell the mice apart, as for any mapping. While it runs Whisker reads the pointer's position about 120 times a second, and while it doesn't, Whisker reads nothing extra. It stores no positions.
  • If you edit the settings file by hand and give Auto-scroll a wheel trigger, or a dead zone or top speed outside the ranges above, Whisker drops that mapping when it loads the file and tells you.

Not yet verified on hardware: how it behaves in TextEdit and in Safari, where nested scrolling means it isn't known which pane receives scrolling sent to the anchor; with several displays; with mixed pointing devices; and how the speeds feel, so the defaults may change.

Click Lock (Experimental)

Click Lock holds the primary button down for you, so you can drag or select without keeping a finger on it. It is experimental, it has not yet been verified on hardware, and it is off until you turn on Enable Click Lock in Settings › General › Experimental. Then give a button the Click Lock (Experimental) action in Settings › Buttons. The action isn't offered while the switch is off, and a mapping that already has it does nothing, and says so, until you turn the switch back on.

Press that button once and Whisker holds the primary button down, so moving the mouse drags. Press it again, or any other button given Click Lock, on any mouse, and Whisker lets go. Letting go of the button itself does nothing, so you don't have to keep holding it. The trigger has to be a mouse button, not a wheel direction, and it can't be the primary button, with or without modifier keys, because that button is how you get out. There is one lock at a time. It is never saved, so a lock doesn't come back when Whisker starts again.

  • Getting out. Pressing the primary button, on any mouse, lets go: Whisker lets go first and the press then reaches the app unchanged, so it also works as an ordinary click. A button, or a wheel direction, mapped to Click on the primary button, Double Click or Triple Click lets go instead of clicking again. Release Click Lock at the top of the menu bar menu lets go too, and is shown only while a lock is held. For a mouse with its primary and secondary buttons swapped, the button that now acts as the primary lets go without clicking, and the physical primary button lets go and then goes through unswapped.
  • While it's held. The menu bar icon becomes a padlock, even if you have hidden the icon, and its tooltip says "Click Lock is on: press the primary button to let go". VoiceOver names the icon "Whisker: Click Lock is on". To turn movement into a drag, Whisker switches on a second event tap, for pointer movement only, while a lock is held. It changes nothing else and stores no positions.
  • Called off. It lets go once, by itself, and never starts again by itself, after a change of app (even if you come straight back), bypass, profile or settings, when its mapping is removed, when Whisker or Click Lock is switched off, when Whisker loses Accessibility access or macOS pauses it, when the Mac sleeps or Whisker quits, when you start Record Button…, and when the mouse that began it is unplugged. When Whisker can't tell for certain which mouse began it, any change to the connected mice lets go.
  • When it won't start. The press and its release are ignored, and nothing is held, if another button is down, Hold to Scroll or a button gesture is under way, the movement listener it needs isn't ready, or the duplicate-click filter held the press back. A press the filter held back can still let go of a lock, so the filter can delay the press that lets go by up to its interval.
  • With other features. Auto-scroll can't start while a lock is held: its button is swallowed and does nothing. Pressing a Click Lock button while an auto-scroll runs stops the auto-scroll first, as any button press does, and then starts the lock. A button gesture can't start while a lock is held, and a Click Lock can't start during one. Click Lock isn't offered as a gesture action, and Whisker removes it from a settings file that names it. Hold to Scroll isn't stopped by a lock, but the two haven't been tried together.
  • Which mouse. It needs Accessibility but no Input Monitoring of its own, and any mouse can start a lock, let go of one or press the primary button to get out. Input Monitoring only lets Whisker tell which mouse began the lock, so that unplugging that mouse lets go.
  • If you edit the settings file by hand and give Click Lock a wheel trigger or the primary button as its trigger, Whisker drops that mapping when it loads the file and tells you. A mapping that has gestures runs only its gestures.

If a button ever seems stuck, press and release the primary button yourself, on any mouse, or choose Release Click Lock in the menu bar menu.

Not yet verified on hardware: that macOS lets Whisker turn pointer movement into a drag and that apps treat it as one, such as drag-selecting text in TextEdit and moving or dragging items in Finder; that the lock's release always reaches an app before the primary press that follows it, since Whisker posts it from inside the event tap; recovery after Accessibility access is revoked or Whisker is force-quit while a lock is held, when Whisker may not be able to let go and you would need to press and release the primary button yourself; how it copes with movement from other mice, several displays, mixed pointing devices or another app that watches pointer movement; whether the duplicate-click filter's delay ever gets in the way of the press that lets go; and how it combines with Hold to Scroll.

Swap primary and secondary buttons

For a mouse set up the other way round, such as for your left hand. Turn on Swap primary and secondary buttons under a mouse in Settings › Devices, or in Settings › Buttons while that mouse's own profile is open, and its primary and secondary buttons trade places: the primary button does what the secondary button does, and the other way round. It is off by default and belongs to that one mouse.

  • The two buttons always swap together. Only plain presses are swapped: a click with ⌘, ⌥, ⌃ or ⇧ held, the middle and other buttons, the wheel, other mice and trackpads are left as they are.
  • A press keeps the button it began as until you let go, even if you change the setting, switch app or turn Whisker off meanwhile, so a click is never left half-finished. If the mouse is unplugged in the middle of a click, Whisker lets the button go.
  • It needs Allow remapping the primary button to be on in Settings › General › Safety, because it changes what the primary button does. Whisker never turns that on for you. While it is off the swap can't be switched on, and one that was already on pauses until you turn it back on.
  • It needs Input Monitoring, even with one mouse, so Whisker knows which mouse you pressed and never swaps a trackpad or another mouse by mistake. It also does nothing while Whisker or that mouse is switched off, or an app on the bypass list is in front.
  • A mapping you made for the plain Primary Button or Secondary Button wins. Where the profile that applies maps either of them, even to Pass through or by inheriting the mapping, the swap leaves both buttons to that profile, and Settings names the profile in the way. If every profile that applies to the mouse does this, Settings says nothing is being swapped.
  • Turning it on starts a 60-second trial, like the duplicate-click filter: unless you keep it, it switches itself off. The buttons have just traded places, so press Keep with the button that now acts as primary, or press Return. Esc or Switch Off turns the swap off at once. Both are ordinary buttons, reachable with the keyboard and VoiceOver, and Whisker asks VoiceOver to announce the trial when it starts.
  • The duplicate-click filter, if you use it, looks at the button you physically pressed, before the swap.
  • Whisker doesn't work out which hand you use, doesn't change any setting in System Settings, and doesn't add two-button chords or middle-click emulation.

Not yet verified on hardware, with VoiceOver or with Full Keyboard Access: how the swap combines with the secondary-click setting in System Settings, so if you use both, check that the buttons end up the way you want; whether every app follows it, since an app that reads the mouse directly may ignore it; and how the trial behaves with VoiceOver and the keyboard.

Scrolling

Settings › Scrolling has settings for the selected profile and for each mouse. The distance settings work on wheels that click from notch to notch; mice that send smooth scrolling keep their own feel.

Direction

Reverse vertical scrolling and Reverse horizontal scrolling are set for each mouse, so a mouse can scroll one way while your trackpad keeps the macOS setting. Reversing works on smooth scrolling too.

Lines per notch

Turn on Set how far each notch scrolls and choose 1 to 20 lines. With it off, each notch scrolls the distance macOS normally uses.

Speed up quick spins makes fast spins travel further. Up to four notches a second, each notch scrolls the number of lines you chose. Faster than that, each notch scrolls more, in proportion to how fast you're spinning, measured over the last fifth of a second. Try it in the scroll playground on the home page.

Scroll sideways while holding a modifier key turns the wheel into a horizontal scroll while that key is down.

Speed while holding keys

Turn on Scroll faster or slower while holding keys to change how far the wheel scrolls while keys you choose are down. Pick the keys, a Speed and what it Applies to: both directions, up and down only, or left and right only. Speeds run from ×0.25 to ×8, so ×0.5 scrolls half as far and ×2 twice as far. Let go of the keys and scrolling goes back to normal. Each profile has its own setting, which replaces Default's along with the rest of its scrolling settings.

  • The speed is applied last, on top of lines per notch, speeding up quick spins and sideways scrolling. Applies to means the directions the app receives, so a wheel turned sideways with the sideways key counts as left and right.
  • A wheel action on the same keys wins: that turn runs the action and isn't scaled.
  • Don't choose exactly the keys that scroll sideways. Whisker warns you and leaves the speed off until one of them changes.
  • The keys held for speed aren't passed on to the app with the scroll, so they don't also trigger its own shortcuts for them, such as zooming. Any other keys you hold still are.
  • At slow speeds a notch can come to less than a line. Whisker keeps the part that doesn't fit and adds it to the next notch in the same direction, so at ×0.25 every fourth notch moves one line. It forgets what is left over when you reverse the wheel, let go of the keys or stop for a second.
  • Like the distance settings, it works on wheels that click from notch to notch. Trackpads and smooth scrolling are left alone.

This has not yet been verified on hardware, including the wheel modes and connection types of mice such as the Logitech Lift and MX Master 3S. The ×0.25 and ×8 limits may change once it has been tried on real mice.

Native wheel settings

Instead of Whisker adjusting each scroll event, you can ask macOS to change the mouse's own wheel speed and acceleration. In Settings › Scrolling, turn on Use this mouse’s own wheel settings for a mouse. It is off by default.

  • It is offered only for a mouse whose current values Whisker can read and put back. Otherwise the option is unavailable, and a mouse that can't be told apart from another of the same kind is left alone.
  • Some mice accept the values but ignore them. Whisker can't check the effect on the wheel, so if nothing changes, turn the option off.
  • While it is on for a mouse, Whisker's lines per notch and acceleration are suspended for that mouse. Direction, sideways scrolling and speed while holding keys still work.
  • Switching it off, switching Whisker off, quitting, or bringing an app on your bypass list to the front puts the mouse's original values back. If Whisker stops unexpectedly, it does this the next time it opens. It doesn't overwrite a change made by something else in the meantime.
  • If any of the values can't be written, read back or restored, Whisker undoes the whole change for that mouse and doesn't retry by itself.

This has not yet been verified on hardware, so the accepted ranges and the real effect on mice such as the Logitech Lift and MX Master 3S are untested. If a mouse won't take its values back, see Troubleshooting.

Smooth notched scrolling

Turn on Smooth notched scrolling in Settings › Scrolling and choose a Duration, from 40 to 400 milliseconds (120 to begin with). It is off by default, and each profile has its own setting, which replaces Default's along with the rest of its scrolling settings.

  • It spreads a distance the wheel already scrolls over time. It adds no distance and no coasting: the total is the same as without it, including the effect of lines per notch, speed while holding keys, reversed direction and scrolling sideways.
  • A wheel action on the same direction and keys still takes priority, and that notch doesn't scroll as well. The smooth sideways stream for Scroll Left and Scroll Right keeps its own handling and isn't smoothed.
  • If you use the mouse's own wheel settings, Whisker spreads the distance the mouse then reports and adds no acceleration of its own.
  • It applies only to notches from a mouse Whisker can identify exactly. Trackpads, mice that already send smooth scrolling, coasting, touch mice and anything Whisker is unsure about are passed through untouched. If Whisker can't take a notch on, it scrolls at once as it would with smoothing off.
  • While Reduce Motion is on in System Settings, smoothing is bypassed, and it stops as soon as you turn it on.
  • Whatever is still pending is dropped, never delayed into something else, when Whisker is switched off, you record a button, the Mac sleeps, Accessibility is lost, a bypassed app or another app comes to the front, the mouse is unplugged, or you start Hold to Scroll. Moving the pointer over another window mid-scroll isn't detected.

Smoothing has not yet been verified in real applications, native or browser: apps differ in how they treat pixel-based scrolling, and some may scroll differently from how they do with notches. Duration and feel are untested on hardware.

Wheel actions

The wheel can trigger actions too. In Settings › Buttons, choose Record Button… and turn the wheel: each direction (up, down, left and right) is its own trigger, with any modifier keys. ⌥ + wheel up for Volume Up is a common one. Most people keep a modifier on wheel mappings so that plain scrolling still works.

Repeat for every notch runs the action once per notch. With it off, the action runs once for each burst of turning.

Smooth sideways scrolling

Some mice, such as those with a thumb wheel, send sideways movement as smooth scrolling rather than notches. Ordinary Scroll Left and Scroll Right mappings only react to notches. On a mapping for one of them, turn on Also act on smooth scrolling from this mouse and choose Activate after, from 10 to 500 pixels (60 to begin with). Whisker then adds up the sideways movement in one direction and runs the action once when it reaches that distance, however finely the movement is reported. The rest of that swipe is absorbed without running it again, and the action can run again after a short pause or when you reverse.

  • Only the sideways movement is taken. Vertical scrolling at the same moment carries on as normal, including your scrolling settings.
  • It needs a mouse Whisker can identify exactly. If Whisker can't tell which mouse a movement came from, or it comes from a trackpad or touch mouse, it passes through unchanged.
  • Coasting after you let go never runs the action, and smooth input is measured in pixels only, so line counts are never mixed in.
  • It is separate from the notch behaviour above and doesn't change it. Whisker can't tell a thumb wheel from another control that reports the same sideways movement, so it doesn't promise to.

This has not yet been verified on hardware, including how the Logitech MX Master 3S thumb wheel reports movement over Bluetooth and a receiver, or what distance feels comfortable.

Pointer

In Settings › Pointer, turn on Custom pointer speed to give a mouse its own feel:

  • Speed, from ×0.1 to ×5.
  • Acceleration, from 0 to 3. The macOS default is about 0.69.
  • Linear (no acceleration), so the pointer moves the same distance however fast you move.

If you more than double the speed, by dragging the slider or switching to a faster preset, Whisker asks Keep this pointer speed? and counts down five seconds. Choose Keep (Return), or choose Revert (Esc) or wait to go back. Use System Settings hands the mouse back to macOS instead. This saves you from a pointer too fast to reach the button.

Whisker applies your settings again when the mouse reconnects. Switching Whisker off, quitting it, or bringing an app on your bypass list to the front puts your Mac's own settings back, and if Whisker ever stops unexpectedly, it puts them back the next time it opens.

Some mice don't accept pointer changes. If so, Whisker shows macOS didn't accept a pointer change for this mouse. If a mouse won't take its own settings back, Whisker says so and keeps trying; see Troubleshooting.

Slow pointer while held

Map a spare button to Slow pointer while held under Settings › Buttons. Choose a factor from ×0.1 to ×0.9 (×0.5 initially). Only the mouse you press slows down. Input Monitoring, exact identification, a supported pointer service and an enabled Custom pointer speed baseline are required; otherwise the button passes through normally. Trackpads, touch mice and indistinguishable mice are not eligible, and the primary-button safeguard still applies.

Saved settings and acceleration stay unchanged. Overlapping holds use the slowest factor rather than multiplying together, within the normal pointer speed bounds. The Pointer pane shows the temporary speed and baseline, explains unavailable states, and has a keyboard-accessible Cancel pointer slowdown button. Releasing restores the latest applicable baseline without a new confirmation. After cancelling, let go before pressing again.

Changing profiles, settings or the app in front, bypass, disconnecting, a paused event tap, sleep, switching sessions, disabling Whisker and quitting all cancel the hold. Reconnection applies the baseline, not a stale hold. Failed restoration is reported; original values are retained for recovery and retry.

This changes macOS pointer response, not sensor DPI. Repeated restoration on real hardware and behaviour in games or raw-input paths are not yet verified; there is no game or anti-cheat compatibility claim. Settings gain an additive action without another schema change. Unknown top-level settings keys are preserved, but unknown nested keys still are not.

Speed presets

Name the speeds you use and switch between them without dragging a slider. With Custom pointer speed on, set the speed and choose Save Current Speed as Preset…. Each mouse keeps its own list of up to 12 presets, shown as Fast — ×1.5, which you can rename or delete from the same pane. A custom speed that matches none of them reads Custom.

  • Switching: choose Use beside a preset in Settings › Pointer, pick it from that mouse's Pointer Speed submenu in the menu bar, or map a button to Pointer Speed Preset. A preset sets the speed only: the mouse's acceleration and Linear setting stay as they are.
  • Software speed, not DPI: a preset is a multiplier on the movement the mouse reports, the same as the speed slider. It doesn't change the mouse's own DPI, which only its maker's software can do.
  • Keep or revert: a switch that more than doubles the speed gets the same prompt as the slider. In Settings, Return keeps it and Esc reverts it; in the menu, choose Keep Pointer Speed or Revert Pointer Speed. With no answer it goes back by itself after five seconds. When a button starts a switch that needs keeping, Whisker opens Settings › Pointer so the keys work.
  • One change at a time: while a change is waiting to be kept or reverted, no other switch is accepted, for any mouse, and the preset rows are greyed out.
  • Recovery: Use System Settings, in the Pointer pane, the menu and the waiting prompt, hands that mouse's speed straight back to macOS. If macOS refuses a switch or takes only part of it, Whisker puts the previous speed back and says so in the Pointer pane. If it can't even do that, it keeps the original values and tries again, as for any other pointer change.
  • When it does nothing: switching is refused while Whisker or the mouse is switched off, while Custom pointer speed is off for the mouse, while it isn't connected (or is one of several identical mice Whisker can't tell apart), and while the app in front is on your bypass list. The Pointer pane says why, and a greyed-out menu row shows the reason when you point at it.
  • Reconnecting: when a mouse comes back, Whisker applies the last speed you kept, never one that was still waiting to be kept.
  • Deleting: deleting a preset also removes the button mappings that switch to it, and Whisker asks first when there are any. Forgetting a mouse under Not Connected in Settings › Devices removes its presets and those mappings too.

Not yet verified on hardware: speed presets have only been tested against a stand-in for the system's pointer settings, not against real mice, so how each mouse responds to a switch hasn't been confirmed. If Whisker is force-quit or crashes within the five seconds, the speed it was waiting on stays saved and applies at the next launch; choose Use System Settings to drop it.

Pointer orientation — foundation only

Not available as a pointer setting. The maintainer decision on rewriting pointer coordinates is pending (issue #32). Tested rotation maths, fractional-count handling and confidence calculations for deliberately recorded forward strokes are in place, but there is no calibration wizard or usable global rotation. Confidence describes agreement between stroke directions, not a probability that a correction is right; the thresholds are provisional and not verified on hardware.

The per-device JSON key rotationDegrees defaults to zero, rejects non-finite numbers and normalises equivalent angles to −180° inclusive through 180° exclusive. It is dormant: editing it does not rotate movement or drags, enable custom pointer speed, change a speed preset, reverse a wheel or alter button identities. The primary-button guard stays unchanged. Unknown top-level settings survive a round trip, but unknown nested keys do not; this part of issue #32 remains unmet.

Before any global control is offered, a public-API prototype must demonstrate exact motion attribution (never inferred from one listed mouse), coherent pointer and drag coordinates across mixed-scale displays and edges, and keyboard cancellation, timeout, disable and interruption recovery without jumps or drift. These checks and raw-input compatibility are unverified. No hardware compatibility is claimed. Repositioning the trackball remains the available workaround.

Profiles

The Default profile applies everywhere. Other profiles apply to an app, a mouse, or one mouse in one app:

  • App profiles: Settings › Applications › App Profiles, then Add Running App or Choose App….
  • Mouse profiles: Make Profile in Settings › Devices.

When you use your mouse, Whisker picks the most specific profile that matches: this mouse in this app, then this app, then this mouse, then Default. The menu shows which one is in use.

A profile only holds what it changes. Its mappings sit on top of Default's: one with the same trigger and modifier keys replaces Default's, and the rest are inherited. To switch off an inherited mapping in one app, map the same trigger to Default. A profile with its own scrolling settings replaces Default's scrolling settings as a whole.

You can delete app profiles; the Default profile stays.

Application bypass

If an app, such as a game, should get your mouse exactly as macOS delivers it, you can tell Whisker to stand aside while that app is in front. Add the app under Settings › Applications › Application Bypass with Bypass Running App or Choose App to Bypass…, or tick Bypass Whisker in this application on an app profile. It covers every mouse, and changes to the list apply straight away.

  • What stops: button remapping, wheel actions and scroll changes. Each mouse's own pointer speed and acceleration come back from the system too, and pointer speed presets can't be switched until you leave the app.
  • What doesn't change: your profiles and the Enabled switch stay as they are. Bypass never turns Whisker on, so if Whisker is off it stays off. The menu shows Bypassed for app separately from Enabled.
  • When the app comes forward: anything Whisker was holding, such as a button held down for a drag or a shortcut that's repeating, is let go once. The real release that follows isn't passed on to an app that never saw the press.
  • When you switch to another app: your latest settings apply again. Anything you edit in the meantime waits until then.
  • If Whisker can't tell which app is in front: an app without a readable identifier changes nothing. Whisker stays out of the way if the app before it was bypassed, and keeps working if it wasn't, until an app it can identify comes forward. Sleep, switching users and quitting an app don't change this either.
  • Permissions: bypass works with or without Input Monitoring, and it doesn't change which permissions Whisker needs.
  • If a mouse won't take its values back: Whisker says so in the menu and in Settings, keeps the record of the original values and tries again, including the next time it starts.

Whisker can see which app is in front, not what a game does with its input. Application bypass isn't a raw-input mode. It doesn't change Game Mode, doesn't make a game accept synthetic clicks or keys, and can't promise compatibility with anti-cheat software. A launcher and the game it starts can be separate apps, and which one is in front while you play hasn't been verified on hardware for particular games, so add whichever one is. Opening a Whisker window brings Whisker to the front, which ends the bypass until you switch back.

Settings file

Everything is saved as plain JSON in:

~/Library/Application Support/Whisker/settings.json

Whisker also keeps a small state.json beside it, which it uses to restore your pointer settings. Saves are written in one go, so a crash can't leave half a file.

  • Settings › General › Settings File has Export…, Import… and Reset to Defaults…, which asks Reset all Whisker settings? first.
  • An import is checked before it's used. If the file isn't valid, nothing changes and Whisker tells you why.
  • Values out of range are repaired when the file loads. Extra keys at the top level of the file are kept.
  • If you edit the file by hand, quit Whisker first and open it again afterwards.

If the file is damaged, Whisker moves it aside as settings.unreadable-date.json and starts with default settings. If the file can't be read at all, was written by a newer version of Whisker, or can't be moved aside, Whisker uses default settings and doesn't save anything until it can, so your file is never overwritten. Settings explains what happened.

Updates

Whisker only checks for updates when you ask. In Settings › Updates, Check for updates automatically can be Never (the default), Daily, Weekly or Monthly. Check Now, or Check for Updates… in the menu, checks straight away. Updates are signed, and Whisker checks the signature before installing one.

  • Downloaded from the Releases page: Whisker updates itself. It asks before downloading an update and again before installing it.
  • Installed with Homebrew: Whisker doesn't update itself, so that Homebrew stays in charge. It shows the command to run, with Copy Command: 
    brew update && brew upgrade --cask whisker
  • Built from source: This copy of Whisker can't update itself. Pull the latest source and build again, or use Open the Releases Page.

Switching an install between Homebrew and direct downloads

Your settings live outside the app, so they carry over either way.

From Homebrew to a direct download

  1. Quit Whisker, then run brew uninstall --cask whisker.
  2. Download Whisker from the Releases page, move it to Applications and open it. It will then update itself.

From a direct download (or your own build) to Homebrew

  1. Quit Whisker and move /Applications/Whisker.app to the Bin.
  2. Run brew install --cask mrashrhodes/tap/whisker. From then on, update with Homebrew.

If macOS asks for Accessibility access again, remove the old Whisker entry from the list and turn on the new one.

Uninstall

  1. Choose Quit Whisker from its menu. Your Mac's own pointer settings come back.
  2. Move Whisker from Applications to the Bin, or run brew uninstall --cask whisker if you used Homebrew.
  3. In System Settings, remove Whisker from General › Login Items, and from Privacy & Security › Accessibility and Input Monitoring with the − button.
  4. To remove your settings too: 
    rm -r ~/Library/Application\ Support/Whisker

Troubleshooting

Nothing changes when I use my mouse

If the menu bar icon is dimmed, check that Enabled is ticked and that the menu doesn't show Grant Accessibility Access…. Then check the profile shown in the menu is the one you edited.

It stopped working after a rebuild or update

macOS may still list an older copy. Remove Whisker from Privacy & Security › Accessibility with −, then add it again.

An action just beeps

The action uses a macOS keyboard shortcut that's switched off. Turn it on in System Settings › Keyboard › Keyboard Shortcuts. A web address or file path that can't be opened also beeps.

Settings for one mouse apply to the wrong one

With more than one mouse connected, allow Input Monitoring so Whisker can tell them apart. See Permissions. Then open the mouse's Details: Attribution shows how sure Whisker is which clicks are its.

Two mice share one set of settings

They report the same vendor and product ids and no serial number, so Whisker can't tell them apart. Each one's Details says so. See Details.

Input timing says there isn't enough movement, or won't start

Press Start and move the mouse in steady circles for the whole sample, without clicking or scrolling. If it won't start, the window says why: Input Monitoring is off, the mouse isn't connected, two identical mice share one listing, or macOS didn't describe the mouse or let Whisker open it. Trackpads and touch mice have no Input Timing…. See Input timing.

Details shows Unavailable for a mouse's buttons

Whisker didn't get a list from macOS. That says nothing about how many buttons the mouse has. Whisker reads buttons and wheels once Input Monitoring is allowed and doesn't ask for it just to show them, and this hasn't been verified on hardware yet. See Details.

My trackpad or Magic Mouse isn't affected

That's deliberate: Whisker leaves trackpads and touch mice alone. Their Details say why. There is no way to turn on Whisker's button mappings for a Magic Mouse: it hasn't been verified that clicks can be told apart from touch gestures, or from another mouse, without disturbing native scrolling and gestures, and no model or macOS version has been checked. Use Apple's Mouse settings for secondary click and gestures.

Lines per notch makes no difference

Your mouse probably sends smooth scrolling rather than notches. Direction settings still apply.

Speed while holding keys makes no difference

Your mouse may send smooth scrolling rather than notches. Also check that the keys aren't the same ones that scroll sideways and that no wheel action is set on them.

Triple Click doesn't select anything

Whisker only sends the three clicks. Whether they select a line or paragraph, or do something else, is up to the app, and some apps don't treat them as a triple click. See Triple Click.

Some of my clicks are ignored

If you turned on Duplicate clicks, a quick deliberate double-click can be ignored. Press Turn Off Filter under the mouse in Settings › Devices, or lower the time.

Swapping the buttons does nothing

The note under Swap primary and secondary buttons says why. It is usually that Allow remapping the primary button is off in General, that Input Monitoring isn't allowed (so Whisker can't tell which mouse you pressed), that Whisker or the mouse is switched off, or that a profile maps the plain Primary Button or Secondary Button itself, which wins over the swap. Clicks with a modifier key held are never swapped. See Swap primary and secondary buttons.

I can't click Keep after swapping the buttons

Click it with the button that now acts as primary, or press Return. Esc turns the swap off at once, and if you do nothing it switches itself off after 60 seconds.

A gesture button does nothing

Check that at least one direction or Tap has an action, and that Input Monitoring is allowed, so Whisker can tell which mouse you pressed (see Permissions). A gesture on the primary button without a modifier key also needs Allow remapping the primary button. A diagonal movement runs nothing, a change of app calls the gesture off, and the button has to reach macOS as an ordinary press and release, which has not yet been verified for any particular mouse. See Button gestures.

Auto-scroll doesn't scroll, or scrolls the wrong thing

Press the button once and let go; holding it does nothing more. It doesn't start while Whisker is switched off, while an app on the bypass list is in front, or during Hold to Scroll, a button gesture or a held Click Lock, and the profile that applies must map that button to Auto-scroll (Experimental). Inside the dead zone nothing scrolls, so move the pointer further from where you pressed. The scrolling goes to the window under the spot where you pressed, not under the pointer as it moves, so stop it and start again with the pointer over the pane you want. If it keeps stopping, something in the list under Auto-scroll is ending it, such as a button press, the wheel or a mouse being connected or disconnected, and it never starts again by itself.

Click Lock doesn't start

Check that Enable Click Lock is on in Settings › General › Experimental, that the profile that applies maps the button to Click Lock (Experimental), and that the button isn't the primary button or a wheel direction. Press it once and let go. It doesn't start while Whisker is switched off, while an app on the bypass list is in front, while another button is down, during Hold to Scroll or a button gesture, or when the pointer-movement listener it needs isn't ready, and then the press and its release are ignored. If it keeps letting go by itself, something in the list under Click Lock is ending it, such as a change of app or profile.

A button seems stuck after using Click Lock

Press and release the primary button yourself, on any mouse, or choose Release Click Lock in the menu bar menu while it's shown. If Whisker was force-quit or lost Accessibility access while a lock was held, it may not have been able to let go, and recovery from that has not yet been verified. See Click Lock.

Pointer speed won't change

Some mice don't accept pointer changes from macOS. Whisker tells you when that happens.

A pointer speed preset won't switch

The preset is greyed out in the menu, or Use is greyed out in Settings › Pointer; point at it to see why. Another speed change may be waiting to be kept or reverted, Whisker or the mouse may be switched off, Custom pointer speed may be off for the mouse, the mouse may not be connected, or the app in front may be on your bypass list. When a switch from a mapped button isn't allowed, Whisker beeps and changes nothing. If macOS refuses the new speed, Whisker puts the previous one back and says so in the Pointer pane. See Speed presets.

The pointer is suddenly too fast

Wait for the countdown: unless you keep it, Whisker goes back to the speed from before the change after five seconds. To go back at once, press Esc in Settings › Pointer or choose Revert Pointer Speed in the menu. Use System Settings hands the mouse back to macOS, and quitting Whisker also puts your Mac's own settings back.

Whisker still changes my mouse in an app I've bypassed

Bypass follows the app that's in front, which for a game may be its launcher or its own program. Add the one that's in front while you play. Opening a Whisker window ends the bypass until you switch back. See Application bypass.

The menu says pointer settings couldn't be restored

macOS wouldn't take Whisker's pointer speed or acceleration off a mouse, so it may still be in use. Whisker keeps the original values and tries again each time the app in front or your settings change, and the next time it starts. Settings › Pointer shows which mouse.

Settings say changes won't be saved

Whisker couldn't read your settings file, so it's protecting it. Check the file's permissions, or move it somewhere safe, then quit and reopen Whisker. See Settings file.
